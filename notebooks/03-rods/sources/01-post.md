AGENTS THAT SEE - AGENTS THAT TALK - Post 3 of 15 - Foundations

Every profession has a smallest complete promise. For a pilot it is the checklist. For an agent it is four lines: Role. Output. Do-Nots. Signal. Write them well and a stranger can predict your agent. Skip them and you have built a colleague who improvises - charming in a person, expensive in a system.

THE 30-SECOND VERSION
RODS is the smallest complete contract an agent can carry. R - the role, in two or three sentences. O - the exact shape of every reply. D - the scope it must never drift into. S - the exact phrase that means "I am done." The Signal is the line the system itself checks - which makes it the line that matters most.

THE IDEA TO STEAL - CONTRACTS BEFORE CAPABILITIES
Give an agent a new power only when its contract is updated to say so. The contract becomes the changelog of trust: every capability your agent has was granted on a line somebody wrote and somebody can audit. No line, no power. This one habit prevents most scope drift before it starts.

WHY THE SIGNAL RULES
- The Signal is what the system checks to stop
- Use a phrase that cannot appear by accident
- "Done" appears in normal prose. Signals do not.
- A mismatched Signal is the number-one runaway-conversation bug

APPLIED POC - LEGAL
Idea: Extract obligations from contract clauses into a fixed schema.
Build: One agent, RODS-written: role = clause analyst; output = party / duty / deadline table; do-nots = no legal advice; signal = EXTRACTION COMPLETE.
Guardrail: Output feeds a lawyer's review queue. It decides nothing.
The win: A second person can parse every reply without reading prose.

WHERE IT BREAKS
- RODS is hand-engineered - no automatic optimisation
- Vague Output fields degrade the whole team's structure
- Do-Nots omitted = agents drift into each other's jobs

TRY THIS WEEK
